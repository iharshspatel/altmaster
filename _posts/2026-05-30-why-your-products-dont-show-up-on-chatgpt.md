<p>
  Quick question: have you ever asked ChatGPT to recommend a product?
  Something like "best minimalist leather wallet under $50" or "a
  sturdy travel backpack for a 10-day trip"?
</p>
<p>
  Millions of shoppers now do this before they ever touch Google. And
  here's the uncomfortable part for merchants: if your store isn't
  showing up in those answers, it's usually not because your product is
  worse. It's because the AI can't read it.
</p>
<p>
  Let's unpack what's actually happening behind the scenes, and the
  surprisingly small fix that gets you back in the game.
</p>

<h2 id="what-ai-sees">What ChatGPT Actually "Sees" on Your Store</h2>
<p>
  <strong
    >AI assistants don't browse your store. They read a text copy of
    it.</strong
  >
  When ChatGPT or Perplexity answers a shopping question, it's not
  scrolling your product page admiring your photography. It's working
  from indexed, text-based data, the same kind of crawled information
  Google has used for years.
</p>
<p>That data is basically four things:</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Product titles</strong> → your headline keywords</li>
  <li>
    <strong>Product descriptions</strong> → the details and use cases
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Structured data</strong> → price, availability, reviews,
    category
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Image ALT text</strong> → the only words attached to your
    photos
  </li>
</ul>
<p>
  Notice what's missing from that list: the images themselves. To an
  AI working from your indexed pages, a gorgeous product photo with no
  ALT text is a blank space. It knows a picture is <em>there</em>. It
  has no idea what's <em>in</em> it.
</p>

<h2 id="why-invisible">Why Your Product Images Are Invisible to AI</h2>
<p>
  For most e-commerce stores, the images do the heavy lifting. The
  photo shows the color, the texture, the fit, the vibe, all the things
  that actually make someone buy. Your description might say "soft
  knit sweater," but the image is what closes the sale.
</p>
<p>
  That's exactly the problem. When the persuasion lives in the image
  and the image has no text, an AI assistant has almost nothing to work
  with. It can't tell that your "soft knit sweater" is an oversized,
  oatmeal-colored, ribbed cardigan with wooden buttons, unless someone
  wrote that down.
</p>

<div class="key-fact">
  <div class="key-fact-icon">🤖</div>
  <div class="key-fact-text">
    <strong>The core problem in one line</strong>
    AI can only recommend what it can read. If your selling points live
    in your photos and your photos have no ALT text, the AI literally
    cannot describe your product, so it recommends a competitor it
    can.
  </div>
</div>

<p>
  This is why two stores selling near-identical products can get wildly
  different AI visibility. The one whose images are fully described
  shows up. The one relying on the photos alone disappears.
</p>

<h2 id="alt-text-bridge">How ALT Text Bridges the Gap</h2>
<p>
  ALT text was built for accessibility, so screen readers can describe
  images to visually impaired shoppers. But it quietly does a second
  job: it's often the <em>only</em> text description an image has. And
  an AI assistant reads it for exactly the same reason a screen reader
  does, because it can't see the picture either.
</p>
<p>Here's the difference in practice:</p>
<div class="alt-example">
  <div class="box bad">
    <span class="box-label">✗ What AI sees now</span>
    <ul>
      <li><span class="sample">(blank)</span></li>
      <li><span class="sample">IMG_4872.jpg</span></li>
      <li><span class="sample">sweater</span></li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="box good">
    <span class="box-label">✓ What AI could see</span>
    <p style="margin: 0">
      <span class="sample"
        >Oversized oatmeal ribbed cardigan with wooden buttons,
        relaxed fit, women's, front view</span
      >
    </p>
  </div>
</div>
<p>
  The right-hand version isn't keyword stuffing, it's just an honest,
  specific description. But to an AI deciding what to recommend, it's
  the entire difference between "I can confidently suggest this" and
  "I have no idea what this is."
</p>

<h2 id="same-as-google">
  Good News: This Is the Same Work Google Already Rewards
</h2>
<p>
  You might be bracing for a whole new playbook for "AI SEO." There
  isn't one. The fundamentals that make a product legible to ChatGPT
  are the same ones Google has rewarded for over a decade: descriptive
  ALT text, clear titles, real descriptions, and clean structured
  data.
</p>
<p>
  The difference is that AI is <strong>less forgiving</strong>. Google
  can lean on hundreds of ranking signals, links, page authority,
  click behavior, to surface a thin page anyway. An AI assistant
  generating an answer has far less to fall back on. If the text isn't
  there, you're simply not in the answer. Blanks and filler that Google
  tolerates can quietly cost you the AI recommendation entirely.
</p>

<h2 id="how-to-fix">How to Make Your Products AI-Visible</h2>
<p>
  You don't need a developer or a new tool stack. Work through these in
  order:
</p>
<ol>
  <li>
    <strong>Fill in ALT text on every product image.</strong> Describe
    what's actually shown, product, color, material, key detail. One
    clear sentence per image.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Write real product descriptions.</strong> Not three words.
    Mention the use case, the material, who it's for. This is the body
    text the AI leans on most.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Keep your titles specific.</strong> "Oatmeal Ribbed
    Oversized Cardigan" beats "Cozy Sweater" every time, for both
    search and AI.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Check what's actually there.</strong> View a product
    page's source and read your <code>alt</code> attributes. Blanks and
    filenames are red flags.
  </li>
</ol>
<p>
  Step one is where most stores have the biggest gap, and the fastest
  payoff. It's also the most tedious to do by hand across a full
  catalog, which is exactly the problem the next section solves.
</p>

  the ones that will already be readable when AI-driven shopping
  becomes the default.
</p>
